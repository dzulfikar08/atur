Jira is built for the problems of large organizations: thousands of engineers, layered permission schemes, release trains with dozens of moving parts. Atur is built for teams that need to keep the whole operation visible in one place — issues, support, CRM, time, and a wiki — without spending weeks on configuration. The choice between them is mostly a question of which set of problems you actually have.

## TL;DR verdict

If your team is under fifty people and your work spans more than issue tracking — you're answering customer mail, managing deals, logging billable hours, or maintaining a small knowledge base — Atur's ceiling is high enough and its setup cost is low enough to be worth a look. If you have hundreds of engineers, strict compliance requirements, or an existing investment in Jira workflows and add-ons that teams depend on, stay with Jira until the complexity gap narrows.

## Where Jira wins

Jira's strengths are real and not worth pretending otherwise. It scales to thousands of users, its permissions model can carve up a single instance by department, geography, and legal jurisdiction, and JQL lets analysts slice work in ways most other tools can't match. The Atlassian ecosystem around Jira — Confluence, Portfolio, Advanced Roadmaps, Automation — is deep enough that entire job titles exist to manage it.

For teams that live in custom fields, bespoke workflows, and strict governance, Jira is the only honest option. The setup cost is weeks, sometimes months, but the payoff is a system that can mirror almost any process, however complicated.

## Where Atur wins

Atur trades enterprise depth for breadth across the things a small operation actually touches. Instead of issues alone, the same workspace holds a support portal with tickets and SLAs, a CRM pipeline board with deal-stage history, per-issue time entries with one-click CSV export, and pages with wikilinks, backlinks, and a knowledge graph.

That matters most at the boundaries. A support ticket that needs engineering becomes an issue in the same board. A deal's next step that depends on a feature ships as a link from the proposal to the roadmap. Time spent on a client request lands on the right deal automatically. In Jira today, each of those is an integration, a sync, and another thing that can break.

Setup reflects that difference. Atur is a single workspace configured in minutes: states, cycles, a support inbox, and a pipeline board come from the defaults. Jira's comparable setup is measured in weeks of admin work and ongoing licensing decisions.

The pricing difference is structural, not just arithmetic. Atur is $9/month for a Starter workspace and $29/month for a Team workspace, no credit card required to try. Jira's cost scales per user, so the final number depends on your headcount and the add-ons your teams rely on. For a ten-person team that needs support, CRM, time, and pages alongside issues, that difference is real at renewal time.

Atur also ships a built-in MCP server with 70+ tools, so an AI agent can triage tickets, draft release notes, and answer "what is blocked?" without a separate integration layer. That is an integration burden Jira does not absorb.

## When Atur is the wrong choice

Atur is the wrong choice when your team is large enough that a single workspace becomes a performance problem, when compliance demands carved-up permissions and audit logs that exceed what a small-tracker provides, or when your team has already built the workflows, custom fields, and reporting that Jira supports and is not willing to rebuild them. Five hundred engineers with deep Jira customization invested is not the market this tool is for.

## Pricing, plainly

Atur is $9/month Starter, $29/month Team, with no credit card required to try the full workspace. Jira's pricing is per user, with the final cost depending on headcount and add-ons — see their site for the current number.
